Why it is here

This is not the kind of project I usually show. I made it public to prove a point: when I connect years of experience in technology and design with AI like Claude Code, I can venture into very different stacks. Lately that includes game development. It began as a volunteer project and it is where two things I love meet: my Catholic faith and my gamer side.

Where it came from

Faith, volunteering and a gamer’s itch

I am a Roman Catholic and I serve in the Pascom (communications ministry) of the Diocese of Santo André: I take photos, help with the live broadcasts of Mass and enjoy making VFX for diocesan projects. One day the question came up: what if I built a game to evangelize the kids in catechesis in a playful, simple way?

Concept

A Catholic village inspired by Chaves

The world is a small village where everybody knows everybody, like the neighborhood of Chaves. There is a square, a parish church, neighbors and characters who each carry a piece of knowledge. I dressed it in Brazilian colonial architecture and aimed for a warm, animated-film look.

The target

Three reference images I put in the project folder on day one.

The result

The same ideas running in real time, in the browser.

It did not start pretty. The first playable had a Roblox-like look with little doll characters, and I said so: the graphics were rough and I did not want that style. That feedback is what pushed the project into Blender.

Characters

A village full of people with something to teach

Each character carries a piece of knowledge and talks in short, simple lines, with the source recorded. They have no proper names on purpose: they are roles, like the catechist, the priest and the sister. And the player is always a child, boy or girl, never an adult.

How the characters were made

Directed by hand, built with Blender and AI

The characters were not downloaded. I directed every one of them, and I connected Blender to Claude Code so the AI could build what I described, round after round, until it looked right.

  1. 1

    Direction

    I define the look, the roles and the proportions: kid-friendly and cartoon, warm colors, a boy or a girl to play, never an adult.

  2. 2

    Blender + AI

    Python scripts run in Blender 4.5 build one continuous body mesh, with hair, clothes and accessories as separate objects, so the game can swap hair color, shirt, skirts, cassocks and the altar server tunic.

  3. 3

    Rig and look

    The mesh is bound to a CC0 skeleton with 9 animations (idle, walk, jog, run, jump, land, talk, interact, dance). Toon shading and outlines in Three.js give the cartoon look.

Your own character

One screen, no steps: boy or girl, skin tone, hairstyle, hair color and shirt, with the character changing live beside the panel.

Gameplay

Learn by exploring, answer by playing

Nine missions, all playable from start to finish. Characters send you to places, each discovery opens a short text with its source, and small activities check what you learned, with no pressure and unlimited tries.

The nine missions

  1. 01

    Getting to know God’s house

    Find 5 places in the church and answer 3 picture questions.

  2. 02

    The objects of the Mass

    Discover 5 liturgical objects and play “which object is it?”.

  3. 03

    Learning to pray

    The Sign of the Cross and the Our Father, then complete the prayer.

  4. 04

    The seven sacraments

    Find 7 stained glasses and sort the sacraments into 3 groups.

  5. 05

    My first day as altar server

    Pick the garment, set the credence table, order the procession, carry the candle.

  6. 06

    Rosary apprentice

    Find 4 mystery plaques and pray one mystery while the beads light up.

  7. 07

    The journey of the saints

    Visit 11 chapels, from Saint Francis to Saint Carlo Acutis and Our Lady.

  8. 08

    Helping others

    Bring bread to a sick neighbor and think through situations of charity and forgiveness.

  9. 09

    The Way of the Cross

    Walk the 14 stations and end in the joy of Easter.

Progress that rewards curiosity

Learning points, 6 levels, 14 medals, a discovery diary with the sources, saved prayers and an unlockable altar server tunic. There are also invitations to live what was learned outside the game, which on purpose give no points.

Accessible by default

Big buttons, keyboard navigation, large text, high contrast, reduced motion and a light mode for simple devices.

Faith content

Every text has a source

This was the rule from the first prompt: all catechetical material must come from the Catechism of the Catholic Church and the Vatican website. I did not want a game that “sounds Catholic”, I wanted one that can be checked.

92

sources checked, one by one, listed in the project docs

40

Catechism paragraphs read in the full text and compared with the in-game summary

Vulgata

Bible passages checked against the Latin Nova Vulgata on the Holy See website

The checks found mistakes, and I fixed them

The Glory Be was citing the Catechism, which does not contain that prayer, so it now cites Rosarium Virginis Mariae. The Creed now follows the Catechism text exactly. For Saint Carlo Acutis I removed two details that no consulted source confirmed.

Honest limit

The game does not replace Mass, the sacraments or in-person catechesis. The content was written from official sources, but a catechist or priest still has to review it before it is used with children, and I say so on the game’s own “parents and catechists” screen.

Mobile

Built to be played sideways

The same game opens in a phone’s browser. Round joystick, a jump button and a talk button, controls sized to the screen height and notch-safe, and a layout that never scrolls. The native iOS and Android versions are what I am coding now.

It adapts to the device

On phones it already starts with fewer effects, and on any device, if it slows down for a few seconds, it lowers the effects by itself, one level at a time. There is also a Light mode in the settings. On my iPhone it runs smoothly.

Under the hood

Technology and where the assets came from

A static site: no build step, no server, no database. Open the page and play.

Engine
Three.js r170 with plain ES modules, written for the browser, plus the glTF loader and post-processing.
3D art
Blender 4.5 LTS driven by Python scripts for the characters and the sacred images, plus CC0 packs from Quaternius for nature, buildings, props and animations.
Look
Toon shading in three light bands, dark outlines on characters and sacred images, warm light and soft shadows.
Performance
Textures reduced to 1024 px WebP with glTF Transform and sharp, automatic quality steps, Light mode. When I measured on my computer: about 16 ms per frame at 1080p in the square, with roughly 1 million triangles.
Audio
More than 440 narrated lines with pt-BR neural voices (AI), one voice per character, plus a soundtrack and footsteps that change with the floor. I built a recording studio page so a human voice can replace any line.
Launch
Hosted on Vercel under aventuracatolica.com.br, with a privacy page covering LGPD and ECA Digital. Progress and nickname stay on the device.
AI workflow
Claude Code with game-development skills, working inside the project folder, running Blender from the command line and testing in a real browser on every round.

Where each asset came from

3D models and animations
Quaternius, CC0: Stylized Nature MegaKit, Medieval Village MegaKit, Fantasy Props MegaKit and Universal Animation Library, downloaded from OpenGameArt.
Characters and sacred images
Original meshes built in Blender with scripts, on top of the Quaternius skeleton. No third-party character model.
Paintings and prints
Saints, Our Lady and the Stations of the Cross come from Wikimedia Commons, in public domain or free licenses, with author, license and page recorded in the credits file.
Texts
Catechism of the Catholic Church, vatican.va and the Nova Vulgata, with each reference stored beside the text that uses it.
Voices
Neural pt-BR voices (AI), replaceable by human recordings.
Libraries
three.js (MIT), glTF Transform (MIT) and sharp (Apache-2.0). Blender (GPL) is used only to generate files and is not shipped with the game.
Process

Built with Claude Code, one round at a time

In its first days the project went through more than twenty rounds. Each round was the same loop: I play, I say what bothers me, Claude changes it, and we test again.

  1. Nov 12

    The brief

    I asked Claude Code to act as a full game-development team (designers, developers, 3D artists, kid UX, writers and catechism consultants) and deliver a real, playable game, not a concept. Browser first, architecture ready for mobile, reference images in a folder.

  2. Nov 12

    “The graphics are still rough”

    The first playable had the structure I liked, but the look was not it. I asked for real saint images, a boy or girl to play (no adults), the Passion of Christ told in a playful way, and a better render.

  3. Nov 12

    Blender comes in

    I told Claude it could install whatever was needed. Blender 4.5 went on the machine, the characters were rebuilt as a skinned mesh, the village got CC0 packs, and toon shading plus outlines moved the look toward the references.

  4. Nov 13

    The official site

    The site goes public on Vercel under the custom domain aventuracatolica.com.br. Parents and catechists screen (LGPD, ECA Digital), the 92-source check, soundtrack, a landscape phone layout and automatic graphics quality.

  5. Nov 14 to 15

    Roles instead of names

    All characters became roles (“Hi, I am the catechist”), and the voices were regenerated. Saint Francis got a statue in the square, with a Dog and a Cat that remind kids animals are God’s creatures (Catechism, n. 2416).

  6. Nov 17

    Polish

    A better face for the Saint Francis statue, moved away from Our Lady’s fountain; the Cat now sits near a neighbor’s house and the Dog wanders the square. Small things that make the village feel alive.
